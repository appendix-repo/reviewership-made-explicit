# Review: "Modularity, Not Mimicry: Interoperability Standards and the Diffusion of IT Outsourcing in U.S. Hospitals"

## Summary

The paper argues that certified interoperability standards made hospital IT modular, which lowered effective asset specificity and widened outsourcing. A threshold model yields two propositions. P1: modularity raises the outsourcing threshold. P2: uncertainty amplifies that effect.

A fixed-effects panel of 412 hospitals (2009–2019) finds three things:
- a positive within-hospital association between modularity and the outsourced share of IT spending (H1);
- a negative modularity × uncertainty interaction, which the authors report as contrary to H2;
- a peer-outsourcing coefficient that falls and loses significance once modularity enters (H3).

The paper concludes that mimicry is "ruled out" and that outsourcing is "economizing".

The question matters, the mechanism deserves attention, and the panel is valuable. But four things fail:
- P2 is mis-derived;
- the test of the rival does not discriminate;
- the causal reading of the modularity effect is not identified;
- neither the mechanism nor the efficiency conclusion is shown.

The first and the last can be repaired within the paper's own framework. The second needs a headline claim withdrawn or a new test. The third needs a design.

## Major concerns

### 1. P2 does not follow; under the paper's own A3 its sign is reversed (damaging)

I re-derived both propositions by hand (§2.2–2.3 and the Appendix), and a symbolic check agrees.
- **P1 follows:** ∂k*/∂m = k*/(1−m) > 0.
- **P2 does not.** The proof treats H as constant in u. With H′ = h1:

  ∂²k*/∂m∂u = [g1h0 − h1(s+g0)] / [H²(1−m)²]

  A3 makes the numerator negative, so the cross-partial is negative at every admissible (m, u). P2 holds exactly when A3 fails. A3, the only numbered assumption, is used in no proof.
- **A change of functional form will not rescue P2.** For any positive H and G, the numerator is G′H − (s+G)H′. That is negative whenever hold-up hazards respond proportionally more to uncertainty than in-house costs do, which is the premise A3 takes from Williamson (1991).

What follows:
- **A3 needs a defence (std:1(b)).** It rests on a citation, not on an argument that it holds for hospital IT. Every corrected sign below depends on it.
- **The step from k* to Φ (std:1(c)).** With Φ = F(k*), where F is the spending-weighted distribution of k:
  - for H1 the step holds: ∂Φ/∂m = f(k*)·∂k*/∂m ≥ 0 for any F. Please say so.
  - for H2 it does not: the interaction on Φ has the sign of [g1h0 − h1(s+g0)] × [f(k*) + k*f′(k*)], so it depends on F. In the uniform and Beta families it reverses only where outsourcing shares exceed roughly 0.6, against a sample mean of 0.31. Still, the paper must state its assumption on F, derive on Φ, or test at the function level.
- **H2 failed as stated (std:2(e)).** The estimate has the opposite sign and its interval excludes zero (95% CI about −0.10 to −0.02).
  - That counts against the model as the paper states it. It does not count against transaction-cost theory more broadly, which does not entail H2.
  - The paper does not say which assumption or step the failure puts in doubt (std:2(b)).
  - The abstract and the conclusion do not report the failure (std:2(a)).
- **The corrected sign is not a confirmation.** A derivation that is repaired after the result is known, and that flips the predicted sign, is a rescue. It can earn credit only through a further implication that the data could reject, and one exists:
  - Under A3, ∂k*/∂u < 0, so ∂Φ/∂u ≤ 0 at every m, whatever F is.
  - Table 2 does not yet bear this out. The point estimate of the uncertainty effect is positive across the observed range of m (about +0.056 at mean m), but imprecise. Its Model 3 main effect is not significant, and its *** is wrong (Minor concerns).
  - Please report ∂Φ/∂u across m with intervals, on a non-negative measure of u. This needs the covariance of the two coefficients, or a centred m.
- **The legitimacy account (§5 ¶2).** It is introduced after the result, uses a mechanism outside the model, and is called "consistent with our theory". So it counts against the model (std:2(c)–(d)). It is also:
  - unnecessary, once P2 is corrected;
  - an institutional mechanism, in a paper that claims to have ruled such mechanisms out and concludes "Its substance is economizing".

  Delete it, or label it a conjecture and give it a test that separates it from the corrected model.

**Where I may be wrong:** H may be meant not to vary with u. But then A3 is empty, and P2 needs a new motivation.

**A test whose result I can't predict:** model function-level outsourcing on k, m and u, using the survey's function-level ratings.

**What would persuade me:** in that model, a smaller modularity effect at high u, together with a negative effect of u.

**Repair:**
- Correct P2 and H2, and say that the corrected sign was derived after the results were seen.
- State F, or test at the function level.
- Report ∂Φ/∂u.
- Drop §5 ¶2.

### 2. The test of the rival does not discriminate (fatal to "Not Mimicry" and "rule out" as worded)

These claims, in the title, the abstract and §5–6, are the paper's headline. They do not stand on this design. The paper itself survives, because H1 does not depend on them. The concern is closed if the claims are narrowed or a discriminating test is added.

§2.4 treats the disappearance of the peer coefficient as a discriminating test (std:3(b)). It is not, for these reasons.

**Absence is not shown (std:4, std:13).**
- The Model 2 peer coefficient, 0.042 (0.038), has a 95% CI of about −0.03 to 0.12, which reaches 85% of the Model 1 estimate.
- Before computing the interval, I set the smallest effect that matters at half the Model 1 estimate, 0.07. That is my choice, and the authors may justify another.
- Equivalence fails at 0.07 (TOST p ≈ 0.24). The smallest bound the estimate could pass is about 0.105, three-quarters of the Model 1 estimate.
- The fall from 0.137 is probably real if the two estimates are positively correlated. So "falls" may be supported, but "disappears" is not. Please report a test of the change.

**Mediation (std:4(c)).** If hospitals imitate their peers by adopting certified interfaces, modularity lies on the imitation path. Conditioning on it then removes the peer coefficient even when imitation drives outsourcing, so the Model 1–2 pattern arises under both accounts. Diffusion of standards by imitation is the rival's strongest form in this setting.

**Shared conditions (std:14(a), (c)).**
- A lagged state mean confounds imitation with state-level conditions, including the interface requirements of §4.
- A lag does not remove conditions that persist. Persistent state shocks alone yield a positive peer coefficient with no imitation (see also the reflection problem; Manski 1993, to verify).
- The authors' own reading of Model 2 ("the common diffusion of standards across hospitals in the same state") concedes that state conditions drive the peer measure.
- Modularity responds to the same conditions.

**Collinearity and noise (std:4(a)–(b)).** No peer–modularity correlation is reported. A mean over about eight sample hospitals per state is noisy, which weakens the peer measure against modularity: it attenuates modest direct effects.

**The rival's construct (std:3(c)).** §1 names peers that hospitals "regard as successful or legitimate". The measure is all other sample hospitals in the state, which are neither referents nor the state's hospital population. My judgement here is provisional: I use the paper's own definition (citing DiMaggio & Powell 1983), and I flag the point for the second reviewer. But the mismatch is with the paper's own statement of its rival.

**The rival in its strongest form (std:3(a)).** The paper's own sources predict patterns that it does not test:
- imitation under uncertainty, which implies a peer × uncertainty interaction;
- two-stage diffusion, in which later adopters are more imitative (Tolbert & Zucker 1983).

In the typology the paper cites, state requirements for certified interfaces are coercive isomorphism. At most, the design speaks to mimetic isomorphism, not to "institutional explanations" in general (abstract, last sentence).

**H3 as stated and as tested.** H3 is stated as an unconditional association, which Model 1 supports. What §4 finds "not supported" is the conditional version in §2.4. Please restate H3 as the proposition tested.

**Inference (std:9).** The peer measure varies by state and year, but the standard errors are clustered by hospital. That probably makes every peer interval above too narrow. Every model includes the peer measure, so please cluster by state, or two ways, with a small-cluster correction. Please also report the number of states and of sample hospitals per state.

So "rule out" and "Not Mimicry" are not supported. Neither is the reading of "fashion" as "economizing" (std:3(e)–(f)). H1 fits both accounts (std:3(d)). The same bar applies to the paper's own account: state requirements could raise outsourcing directly, not only through modularity (Major 3).

**Where I may be wrong:** the second reviewer may accept the state peer set as the rival's construct.

**A test whose result I can't predict:**
- (i) whether peers' adoption of certified interfaces predicts a hospital's own change in modularity;
- (ii) a peer group that crosses state lines (system, referral or shared-vendor ties), estimated with state-by-year fixed effects.

**What would persuade me:** no effect of peers on a hospital's own modularity, and an interval on the cross-state peer coefficient that excludes 0.07. That would justify "no evidence of direct imitation through this channel".

**Repair:** retitle, and say in the abstract and §5–6 only that the evidence does not require mimicry, unless the tests above are added.

### 3. The causal reading of the modularity effect is not identified (damaging)

**Direction (std:11).** Nothing argues that modularity was not chosen with outsourcing in view.
- Hospitals planning to outsource may install certified interfaces first, and vendors taking over a function may install them. Do vendors do this?
- If they do, same-year feedback alone could produce a positive estimate with no true effect, and lagging m would not solve it.
- Please test whether past outsourcing predicts modularity, and whether, at function level, m rises before or after a function is outsourced.

**Time-varying confounders (std:11(c)).** Year effects absorb the federal EHR incentive programme only if its effects were common across hospitals. Attestation timing varied, and it plausibly moved both certified interfaces and vendor contracts.

**The staffing control (std:7(b), std:10).**
- In-house staffing is partly the outcome, since §3.2 says outsourcing transfers or eliminates the positions.
- When capability drives both modularity and outsourcing, conditioning on staffing can raise or lower the modularity coefficient. Its survival in Model 4 therefore says nothing about capability, and "cannot be attributed to capability differences" (§3.3, §4) does not follow.
- Use capability measured before treatment, such as 2009 staffing or IT budget, interacted with year.

**Controls (std:7).** Please report the key coefficients without the controls, so that readers can see which controls carry identifying weight. The specificity rating is treated under Major 4.

**Variation and comparison (std:10(a), (b), (d), (f)).** Identification comes from within-hospital changes in m.
- Please report how much variation in m remains after the fixed effects, and which hospitals supply it.
- Are hospitals with small changes a credible comparison for hospitals with large ones? Please show whether the two groups differed in outsourcing trends before the changes.

**The event study (std:10(e), std:13).** It is a reduced form of state requirements on outsourcing, with no first stage on m, and none of it is reported. Please:
- report the event-time coefficients with intervals, from an estimator robust to effects that differ by adoption cohort, against not-yet-treated states (Callaway & Sant'Anna 2021; Sun & Abraham 2021; both to verify);
- cluster by state, with a wild bootstrap;
- show leads that are flat and precise, meaning their intervals exclude pre-trends of half the post-adoption effect, or else report a sensitivity analysis (Rambachan & Roth 2023, to verify).

**The requirements as an instrument (std:11(b)).** This is the paper's best route to identification, but only if two conditions hold:
- **Relevance:** report the first stage.
- **Exclusion:** the requirements must affect outsourcing only through modularity, and the paper must argue this. I see three threats to it:
  - requirements may change vendor supply, and so the production-cost advantage s, directly;
  - compliance burdens may push hospitals to outsource directly;
  - the requirements are coercive pressure in the institutional sense (Major 2).

Without exclusion, the event study estimates the effect of mandates, not of modularity.

**Specification (std:10(g)).** Please say how the main specification was chosen, and report the specifications that did not work.

**Where I may be wrong:** some of this may have been done but not reported, and vendors may not install interfaces on takeover in this setting. A reader who admits only design-based evidence would rate this concern fatal. I do not, because the association is sound and the requirements may supply a design.

**A test whose result I can't predict:** 2SLS of outsourcing on modularity, instrumented by the state requirements.

**What would persuade me:** a strong first stage, an argued exclusion restriction, an IV estimate of the same sign, and flat, precise leads.

**Repair:** a design, either an instrument with exclusion argued or timing evidence, plus capability controls measured before treatment. Until then, please use associational language throughout, including in §5.1's advice to practice.

### 4. The mechanism and the efficiency conclusion are not shown (damaging)

**The hold-up channel is not visible in Table 2 (std:3(g)).**
- In the theory, modularity works by lowering effective specificity, k(1−m).
- The specificity rating asks how costly it would be to transfer the hospital's IT functions to a different provider. That reads as effective, switching-cost specificity.
- If it is, m should lower it within hospitals, and β1 conditional on it should be near zero. Neither shows:
  - β1 stays sizeable with the rating controlled.
  - The rating's coefficient barely moves when m enters (−0.094 to −0.091). By the omitted-variable formula, that means m moves by only about 0.03 across the rating's full range, conditional on the controls (recomputed by hand; indicative only, since within-hospital spreads are not reported).
- So either the rating measures something else, or modularity works through a channel the model lacks. One candidate is economizing but not transaction-cost economizing: certified interfaces may widen vendors' scale advantage s.

**The rating (std:7(a)–(c)).**
- Its referent is undefined: nominal or effective specificity, and in-house or outsourced functions.
- For outsourced functions it includes lock-in created by outsourcing itself, so it is partly an outcome (std:7(b)).
- It is a perceptual, single-informant rating without validation (std:7(c)).

Controlled throughout, it is either a mediator or a bad control. Please define it, report the within-hospital regression of the rating on m, and report H1 with and without it.

**Scope of m.** m covers clinical systems only, while Φ covers all functions, including hosting and help desk, which clinical interface standards should not touch.
- A function-level test should focus on the functions the mechanism reaches, and use the others as a placebo for the hold-up channel.
- A modularity effect in commodity functions would fit both the production-cost channel and an institutional bundle of standards and outsourcing. So the function-level test separates hold-up from both of them, but not the two from each other.
- Cost consequences by adoption timing, or whether m rises only in the functions that were outsourced, could separate them.

**"Economizing" (std:8).** Three phrases are efficiency claims: "economizing" (abstract, §6), "efficiency-enhancing" (§5.1) and "the conditions that make it efficient" (§6).
- They are stated as what the results show, so they need either a selection argument or direct evidence on costs.
- Selection is weak among non-profit and public hospitals, and the paper shows no cost, quality or hold-up outcome.
- A fit between choices and transaction attributes supports an alignment claim (choices track specificity), not the claim that the practice economizes.

Show the cost consequences, or restate the claims as alignment.

**Wording cuts both ways (std:12(d)).** "Fashion" mixes motive with outcome. Mimetic isomorphism, as cited, is about motive.

**Where I may be wrong:** the rating may measure nominal specificity. Its stability would then be expected, and the mechanism test would need another measure.

**A test whose result I can't predict:** the within-hospital regression of switching-cost specificity on modularity.

**What would persuade me:** a clear negative effect of m on effective specificity, and an H1 coefficient that shrinks when specificity enters.

**Repair:** the mechanism test, and "economizing" restated as alignment unless cost evidence is added.

## Minor concerns

- **N (std:6(c)).** 412 × 11 = 4,532, not 4,712. If the lagged peer measure also drops 2009, N should be 4,120. Please reconcile N with the sample and the lag, and confirm the estimation sample.
- **Stars (std:6(b)).** Model 3 uncertainty, 0.084 (0.051), is starred *** at p ≈ 0.10. The other 15 cells are consistent. Intervals would serve better than stars.
- **Magnitude (std:6(e)).** "Strongly predicts" (abstract) describes 2.7 points per SD of m, about 0.16 SD of Φ.
  - Both figures use pooled SDs, though identification is within hospitals. Please give magnitudes with intervals.
  - Please also say how much of the decade's doubling (§1) is accounted for by β1 times the change in mean m. Year effects absorb the rest of the field-level diffusion that the abstract asks about.
- **Marginal effect of modularity (std:6(d)).** It is 0.118 − 0.061u in Model 3, reaching zero near u = 1.9 SD (1.7 SD in Model 4). Please report the distribution of u and the covariances.
- **Uncertainty construct.** Admissions volatility is demand volatility, whereas the model's u is disturbances that require adapting the IT transaction. Please argue the link, or add an IT-specific measure.
- **Model domain.**
  - As constructed, u is a z-score, while the model needs u ≥ 0. Below u = −h0/h1 the hazard H is negative and the buy rule reverses.
  - k* is undefined at m = 1, which the data may reach.
  - A3 is the only numbered assumption.
- **Single informant.** One CIO reports m, the rating and, it seems, Φ. For factual items that is acceptable, but reporting can shift when the CIO changes. Please control for CIO turnover, or validate m or Φ against another record. To its credit, the modularity item records use (systems that "exchange data" through certified interfaces), not just certification.
- **Survival selection.** Under fixed effects it matters only if exit tracks changes in modularity or outsourcing, so please report the exclusions and their outcome trends before exit.
- **Practice (§5.1).** The advice to "attend first to the modularity of their systems" presupposes the causal effect and a benefit from outsourcing. Please condition it, or drop it.
- **Readability (std:16).**
  - A3 should be stated in words, with why it signs each prediction.
  - The intuition after P2 describes the per-function cost saving (h1k, which rises with u), not the threshold (which falls with u under A3). It would mislead a non-economist.
  - On whether the rival is recognisable to sociologists, I defer to the second reviewer.
- **Priority and novelty.**
  - If I recall correctly, Loh and Venkatraman (1992, *Information Systems Research*) contrasted imitative and other influences on the diffusion of IT outsourcing. Ang and Cummings (1997, *Organization Science*) examined institutional and efficiency influences on IS outsourcing. Both are to verify.
  - That specificity is partly a product of interface design is already in the paper's own sources (Langlois 2002; Sanchez & Mahoney 1996). The contribution is the formal link and the hospital evidence, and its premise is untested (Major 4).
- **Code and data.** Please supply code that reproduces Table 2 and the event study, and the data, or an access route where the licence allows.

## What survives

- A within-hospital association between modularity and outsourcing, stable across Models 2–4 and correctly reported, in an unusually long panel. Its causal reading is open.
- P1, and the step from P1 to H1, which holds for any spending distribution.
- A model that, once corrected, makes sharper predictions than the paper claims: a negative interaction on the threshold, and a negative effect of uncertainty. Both are testable at the function level.
- A possible route to identification in the state requirements, if exclusion can be argued.
- Good practice: a rival and a test criterion stated in advance, and a contrary result reported openly.

Without this paper we would lose a clear, formal transaction-cost account of how standards shape the boundaries of the firm, in a setting where that matters.

## Recommendation

Major revision. These changes would change my view:
- the adjudication claim withdrawn (title included), or supported by a discriminating test;
- P2 and H2 corrected, with F stated or a function-level test, and §5 ¶2 dropped;
- a design for identification, with capability controls measured before treatment;
- the mechanism tested, and "economizing" restated as alignment or supported by cost evidence.

If the title claim is kept without a discriminating test, I would recommend rejection.
