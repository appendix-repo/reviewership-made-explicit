## 4. Draft standards (16)

For each standard, please say **accept**, **reject** or **rewrite** (and give your wording). Each has a proposed priority: must, should or could. In Round 3, I will make each standard checkable and point out any that pull against each other.

### Inferential

`std:1` **Hypotheses that test a theory must be derived from it.**
- **Standard.** A hypothesis offered as a test of the theory must follow from the theory's stated assumptions. For an interaction, the paper states and defends the assumption that fixes its sign. A hypothesis that does not follow is labelled exploratory, and it counts neither for nor against the theory.
- **Your words.** "A hypothesis that does not follow from the theory cannot test it." (`rv:S1.2`); "the sign has to be derived, not asserted" (`rv:S1.4.3`).
- **Origin:** reviewer. **Proposed priority:** must, because it was your decisive reason in `rv:S1.2`.

`std:2` **Failed predictions are results; after-the-fact rescues are costs.**
- **Standard.** A prediction that follows from the theory and fails is reported as a result, and its consequence for the theory is drawn out. An explanation added afterwards, using a mechanism the theory does not contain, is labelled as such and counted against the theory, not for it.
- **Your words.** Both of the views you drew from the case in `rv:S1.2`. You noted that this sits close to one of the editor's concerns. I have recorded it as your long-held view, not as a verdict on this paper.
- **Origin:** reviewer. **Proposed priority:** must.

`std:3` **Ruling out a rival needs a discriminating test.**
- **Standard.** To claim that a rival account is ruled out, the paper needs a test on which the two accounts predict different things, with the rival's key construct measured in a way its proponents would accept. Evidence consistent with the paper's own account supports it only where the rival predicts something different.
- **Your words.** "To rule out a rival, you need a test on which the two accounts predict different things" (`rv:S1.4.5`); "Consistency with economizing counts as evidence for it only where the rival predicts something different" (`rv:S1.4.4`); `rv:EB.3`.
- **Origin:** reviewer. **Proposed priority:** must.

`std:4` **A lost significance star is not a null result.**
- **Standard.** A coefficient that loses significance when a correlated regressor is added is not evidence that the variable has no effect. Before drawing that conclusion, the paper addresses collinearity, differential measurement error and mediation.
- **Your words.** "The loss can reflect collinearity, differential measurement error (the better-measured variable wins), or mediation" (`rv:S1.4.5`).
- **Origin:** reviewer. **Proposed priority:** must.

### Ontological

`std:7` **Measures fit their constructs for the main variables.**
- **Standard.** The fit between measure and construct is argued, not assumed, for the main regressor and the outcome. An attribute that bears on governance, such as asset specificity, is not inferred from the outcome it is meant to explain.
- **Your words.** "a bad measure there is an identification problem" (`rv:S1.3`); "especially when specificity is measured loosely or inferred from the outcome" (`rv:S1.4.4`).
- **Origin:** reviewer. **Proposed priority:** must. Should this extend to controls? See ONT.4.

`std:8` **Economizing claims must show selection.**
- **Standard.** A claim that observed governance is economizing (that is, efficient, or an optimal response) must show, or argue for, the selection pressure that would weed out misaligned forms: competition and time. Where selection is weak, as in nonprofit or regulated sectors, it cannot be assumed.
- **Your words.** "Where selection pressure is weak, as with nonprofits or regulated sectors, it can't be assumed and must be argued each time" (`rv:S1.4.2`).
- **Origin:** reviewer. **Proposed priority:** should.

`std:9` **[suggestion] Unit of theory and unit of test.**
- **Standard.** If the theory predicts at one level and the test is run at another, the paper shows how the prediction carries over to the tested level. That includes the sign of any interaction on the scale on which it is tested. (In transaction-cost economics, for example, the theory is about individual transactions, but tests are often run on organization-level aggregates.)
- **Why a demanding reviewer in your field would hold it.** Williamson takes the transaction as the basic unit of analysis (Williamson 1985, to verify), so a firm-level test needs an aggregation step.
- **Disclosure.** This bears on the editor's concern `bs:EB.K1`.
- **Proposed priority:** should.

### Epistemological

`std:10` **Find the source of the identifying variation.**
- **Standard.** The paper shows four things: what the fixed effects absorb; which units supply the variation; whether those units differed from the others before treatment; and that the comparison group makes sense. Where units adopt at different times, it shows estimates in event time, with leads, against units not yet treated.
- **Your words.** "I went to the source of the variation, not down the list of robustness checks" (`rv:S1.1`); "an event-study plot with leads and a comparison against firms that had not yet switched" (`rv:S1.1`); your list of what you never skip (`rv:S1.3`).
- **Origin:** reviewer. **Proposed priority:** must.

`std:11` **Direction of causation for attributes chosen together with governance.**
- **Standard.** Where governance is regressed on an attribute that firms may choose together with governance (asset specificity is the standard case), the paper argues for or identifies the direction of causation. Reverse causality and the obvious omitted variables are addressed.
- **Your words.** "Regressing governance on specificity assumes a direction of causation, and the paper must either argue for it or identify it" (`rv:S1.4.1`); your list of what you never skip (`rv:S1.3`).
- **Origin:** reviewer. **Proposed priority:** must.

`std:12` **Symmetry.**
- **Standard.** The review holds the paper's own transaction-cost account to the same falsifiability standard as the rival. Economizing is not treated as the default explanation. The reviewer checks the draft for a tendency to find institutional accounts hard to falsify.
- **Your words.** "I hold my own tradition to the same falsifiability standard I apply to institutional accounts"; "That's a bias of mine, and you should hold me to it" (`rv:S1.4.4`).
- **Origin:** reviewer. **Proposed priority:** must. This standard applies to your review, not to the paper. Mode C will check it.

`std:13` **[suggestion] Claims of absence need bounds.**
- **Standard.** To conclude that a variable has no effect, the paper reports the estimate with its confidence interval and shows that effects large enough to matter are excluded. One way is an equivalence test against a stated smallest effect of interest.
- **Why.** `std:4` says what does not show a null result. This standard says what would.
- **Disclosure.** This bears on the evidence side of the editor's concern `bs:EB.K2`.
- **Proposed priority:** should.

`std:14` **[suggestion] Peer influence versus shared conditions.**
- **Standard.** A measure of peers' earlier adoption counts as evidence of imitation only if the design separates imitation from peers responding in the same way to shared conditions, such as common shocks or common regulation.
- **Why.** This is the standard identification problem for peer effects (Manski 1993, the "reflection problem", to verify).
- **Disclosure.** This bears on `bs:EB.N3` and on a detail the brief reports. Reject it if it reads as fitted.
- **Proposed priority:** should.
