# Referee report (draft, unsigned)

**Manuscript:** "Modularity, Not Mimicry: Interoperability Standards and the Diffusion of IT Outsourcing in U.S. Hospitals"
**Task:** T5, single pass, style S1. No harness was run, so every formal or numerical finding below is `asserted`. Loci: "§" = section, "eq." = displayed equation, "T2" = Table 2. Finding ids: `cmt:M1` to `cmt:M9` (major), `cmt:m1` to `cmt:m10` (minor).

## 1. Summary

The manuscript asks why outsourcing of hospital IT functions spread after 2009. It sets a transaction-cost account, in which interoperability standards make IT functions modular and lower the effective specificity of hospital–vendor relationships (§1; §2.2), against an institutional account in which hospitals imitate peers under uncertainty (§2.4, H3). The model gives a threshold $k^*(m,u)=(s+G(u))/(H(u)(1-m))$ below which functions are bought (§2.3). Proposition 1 says the threshold rises in $m$; Proposition 2 says the effect of $m$ rises in $u$. The empirics use a balanced panel of 412 hospitals, 2009–2019, with hospital and year fixed effects (§3). T2 reports a positive modularity coefficient in every specification that includes it, a negative modularity × uncertainty interaction (Model 3, $-0.061^{**}$), and a peer coefficient that falls from 0.137 to 0.042 and loses significance once modularity enters.

The claimed contributions are (C1) a formal link between modularity and make-or-buy with two propositions (§1); (C2) a test against the institutional rival in a decade-long panel; and (C3) that once modularity is measured, peer effects disappear, so that mimicry is "ruled out" (Abstract; §5).

The question matters. The design, which sets a named rival against the proposed mechanism in nested specifications, is the right kind of design, and the H1 association is plausible in sign. But C1 fails on the paper's own assumptions (`cmt:M1`), C3 rests on a non-significant coefficient and a conditioning strategy that presupposes its conclusion (`cmt:M4`), and the sample count does not reconcile with the stated design (`cmt:M8`). I do not think the conclusions stand in their current form.

## 2. Major concerns

**M1. Proposition 2 is false as proved, and the paper's own Assumption A3 implies the opposite sign.** Severity: `fatal` (for Proposition 2 and H2 as stated).

- *Commitment.* Proposition 2 asserts $\partial^2 k^*/\partial m\,\partial u>0$ (§2.3). The Abstract predicts the modularity effect "is stronger where environmental uncertainty is high". The appendix proof gives $\partial^2 k^*/\partial m\,\partial u = G'(u)/(H(u)(1-m)^2)$.
- *Gap.* Write $k^*=A(u)/(1-m)$ with $A=(s+G)/H$. Then $\partial^2 k^*/\partial m\,\partial u = A'(u)/(1-m)^2$, where $A'(u)=\{G'(u)H(u)-(s+G(u))H'(u)\}/H(u)^2$. The proof keeps only the first term. Its justification, that modularity "operates on the denominator of $k^*$ only through the factor $(1-m)$", does not license dropping $H'(u)$, because $H(u)$ is also in the denominator. With $G=g_0+g_1u$ and $H=h_0+h_1u$, the bracket reduces to $g_1h_0-h_1(s+g_0)$, and the $u$-terms cancel. The cross-partial is therefore positive if and only if $h_1/h_0<g_1/(s+g_0)$, which is the reverse of A3 (§2.2, eq. A3: "$h_1/h_0 > g_1/(s+g_0)$"). Under A3 it is negative for every $u$. The verbal gloss on A3 ("hold-up hazards respond more strongly to uncertainty ... in proportional terms") gives the same condition, since $A'/A=G'/(s+G)-H'/H$. Numerical instance: $s=g_0=g_1=h_0=1$ and $h_1=2$ satisfy A3 and give $A'(0)=-3$. The appendix formula gives $+1/(1-m)^2$; the correct expression gives $-3/(1-m)^2$.
- *Repair.* The authors must choose. (i) Keep A3, correct Proposition 2 to a negative interaction, and revise H2, the Abstract and §1. The T2 interaction is then what the model predicts, and the legitimacy account in §5 is unnecessary (`cmt:M9`). (ii) Keep H2 and replace A3 with the reverse inequality, justifying it against the Williamson (1991) rationale in §2.2, which points the other way. Either route needs a corrected appendix. A check that would settle the algebra (not run):

```python
import sympy as sp
m,u,s,g0,g1,h0,h1 = sp.symbols('m u s g0 g1 h0 h1', real=True)
G = g0 + g1*u; H = h0 + h1*u
kstar = (s + G)/(H*(1 - m))
num = sp.simplify(sp.diff(kstar, m, u)*H**2*(1 - m)**2)
print(sp.simplify(num - (g1*h0 - h1*(s + g0))))   # expect 0
```

**M2. The propositions concern a per-function threshold, but the hypotheses concern spending shares, and the text moves between the two without a mapping.** Severity: `damaging`.

- *Commitment.* §2.3: "where uncertainty is high, the hold-up hazards that modularity neutralizes are most costly, so the value of modularity is greatest." §2.4: Proposition 2 "implies that this association is stronger" for the spending share $\Phi$.
- *Gap.* The intuition is right about a different object. The net advantage of buying a function of specificity $k$ is $NB=C_M-C_B=s+G(u)-H(u)k(1-m)$ (§2.2). Then $\partial NB/\partial m=H(u)k$ and $\partial^2 NB/\partial m\,\partial u=h_1k>0$: for a given function, the gain from modularity does rise with uncertainty. The threshold, which decides which functions are bought, depends on a ratio, and its response is governed by the sign issue in M1. These are different claims. Second, $\Phi$ is the spend-weighted share of functions with $k<k^*$, so $\Phi=F(k^*)$ for the spend-weighted distribution $F$ of $k$. The model never specifies $F$. Then $\partial^2\Phi/\partial m\,\partial u=f'(k^*)\,k^*_u\,k^*_m+f(k^*)\,k^*_{mu}$ (asserted). Under A3 the second term is negative and the first has the sign of $-f'(k^*)$. The share-level interaction is therefore negative when the spend density is non-decreasing at the threshold, and otherwise undetermined. Spend weights may also respond to $m$, which the model ignores.
- *Repair.* State which object each proposition concerns. Specify $F$ (for example, a Beta distribution for $k$ with spend weights), derive the share-level predictions explicitly, and simulate over $F$ to see whether the T2 sign is what the model implies at the share level. Alternatively, state H1 and H2 in terms of the net advantage and test them on function-level data if these exist.

**M3. The paper does not explain the phenomenon it sets out to explain.** Severity: `damaging`.

- *Commitment.* The question is why outsourcing "spread so rapidly" after 2009 (Abstract, first sentence). The Introduction says the outsourced share "roughly doubled" (§1, first paragraph).
- *Gap.* The specification includes year fixed effects, which absorb the aggregate trend (§3.3). By construction the regressions explain within-hospital and cross-sectional variation, not the rise in the mean. The paper never shows the time path of mean outsourcing or mean modularity, and never decomposes the rise. The model is static, with no diffusion or network dynamics (`cmt:m5`), so it does not explain diffusion in the sense of the title either.
- *Repair.* Report the time series of mean $\Phi$, mean $m$ and mean peer outsourcing. Decompose the 2009–2019 change in mean $\Phi$ into the part attributable to the change in mean modularity (coefficient times change in mean $m$), the part attributable to peers, and a residual, with uncertainty. This decomposition is what the title and Introduction need.

**M4. The rejection of mimicry does not follow from a non-significant coefficient or from the chosen conditioning set.** Severity: `fatal` for the headline claim that mimicry is "ruled out", as currently supported.

- *Commitment.* "H3 is not supported" (§4). "The results rule out mimetic isomorphism as a driver of IT outsourcing in this setting" (§5; similar in the Abstract). "Its substance is economizing" (§6).
- *Gap.* (i) Absence of significance is not evidence of absence. In Model 2 the peer coefficient is 0.042 (SE 0.038). Its approximate 95% interval is $-0.03$ to $0.12$, which includes effects of 0.10 or more, most of the Model 1 coefficient (0.137). The data do not exclude a substantial peer effect. An equivalence test, or a stated smallest effect of substantive interest, is required. (ii) Conditioning on modularity biases the test toward the authors' conclusion if mimicry operates through modularity. If hospitals adopt certified interfaces because peers do, modularity mediates the peer effect, and controlling for it absorbs the effect the test is meant to detect. The paper's own account of the peer coefficient ("reflects the common diffusion of standards across hospitals in the same state, not imitation", §4, H3) is exactly this channel, and it is asserted, not measured. (iii) The institutional account's distinctive prediction, as the authors state it, is that organizations "model themselves on others" under uncertainty (§2.4). The paper never tests peer outsourcing × $u$. (iv) Peer outsourcing is a state-year average. State-level supply shocks, such as vendor entry or regional procurement, raise both peer and own outsourcing with no imitation or modularity channel, and year fixed effects do not absorb them. (v) The peer group is "the same state" (§3.2). The field relevant to imitation need not be the state, and the paper does not justify the choice.
- *Repair.* (a) Add peer modularity (state-year mean, lagged) and report whether the Model 1 peer coefficient survives. (b) Test whether lagged peer outsourcing predicts a hospital's subsequent change in modularity. This is the decisive test of the mediation channel. (c) Test peer outsourcing × $u$. (d) Add state-year supply controls where observable, for example the number of vendors active in the state (availability to be verified). (e) Repeat with alternative peer definitions, such as hospital referral region or system peers. (f) Report the interval and an equivalence test, and revise "rule out" to what the evidence supports.

**M5. The causal reading of modularity is not established.** Severity: `damaging`.

- *Commitment.* "Identification therefore comes from within-hospital changes in modularity over time" (§3.3). The event study is "consistent with a causal effect of modularity" (§4, Robustness).
- *Gap.* Hospital fixed effects remove only time-invariant differences. Reverse causation is plausible: a hospital that outsources hosting, application management or revenue-cycle systems may have to expose certified interfaces to the vendor, so outsourcing could raise measured modularity. A common time-varying cause is also plausible, such as an EHR replacement, a platform migration or a new CIO, each of which changes both interfaces and sourcing. The event study has further problems. (i) Only a reduced form (outsourcing on state adoption timing) is reported; no first stage shows that the state requirement raised modularity. (ii) No coefficients, figure, number of states or control group (never-treated or not-yet-treated) are given. (iii) Two-way fixed effects with staggered adoption can mis-weight comparisons when effects vary over time or across cohorts (Goodman-Bacon, 2021; Callaway and Sant'Anna, 2021; Sun and Abraham, 2021). (iv) The exclusion restriction is doubtful: state requirements may change vendor supply or state procurement and so change outsourcing without changing modularity. (v) The requirement itself is not described: what it mandates, which standard, and whether it is a state rule or a federal certification passed through.
- *Repair.* Report the first stage and the reduced form, with coefficients and an event-study figure. Use a heterogeneity-robust estimator and show pre-trends formally. Describe the requirement. Test whether lagged modularity predicts later outsourcing and whether lagged outsourcing predicts later modularity, in a cross-lagged panel, and state what each result would imply. Qualify the causal language until these are in place.

**M6. The controls include variables the outcome may affect, and the capability check cannot do the work claimed.** Severity: `damaging`.

- *Commitment.* In-house staffing "tracks the scale of the hospital's internal IT capability" (§3.2). The authors add it to rule out capability confounding: "If the modularity effect survives this control, it cannot be attributed to capability differences" (§3.3).
- *Gap.* The authors' own §3.2 says that outsourcing a function transfers its staff to the vendor. Staffing is therefore partly determined by the dependent variable. Conditioning on it removes part of the outcome's variation, and its coefficient (−0.143, T2 Model 4) is partly mechanical. A surviving modularity coefficient therefore does not show that capability is not a confounder. Other controls have the same problem. Operating margin is plausibly affected by cost savings from outsourcing, and system membership can change with consolidation. The CIO's rating of the cost of transferring "the hospital's IT functions" (§3.2, asset specificity) is given after contracting and may reflect past outsourcing. It is not the ex ante $k$ of the model.
- *Repair.* Use capability measures dated before the sample period or otherwise unaffected by outsourcing, for example in-house application counts or pre-period IT budget per bed. Report results with and without staffing, margin and asset specificity, and treat the last as a potential outcome. Add an explicit bad-control analysis.

**M7. Construct validity: the measures are not the constructs of the model.** Severity: `damaging`.

- *Commitment.* The model's $m$ is the degree to which a hospital's IT functions interact through standard interfaces (§2.2). Its $u$ is environmental uncertainty that raises both $H$ and $G$ (§2.2). Its $k$ is the nominal specificity of each function (§2.1).
- *Gap.* (i) $m$ is measured as the share of clinical information systems with certified interfaces (§3.2). Several model functions, such as infrastructure hosting and help desk, have no clinical-system counterpart, so the measure covers a subset of the functions the model is about. (ii) The model requires $m<1$ for $k^*$ to be finite. The data have mean 0.46, SD 0.24 and no reported maximum, and the treatment of $m=1$ is not stated. (iii) The model's $u$ is uncertainty about the need for adaptation. The measure is the coefficient of variation of admissions, which is demand volatility. The Discussion then reads the interaction through volatile demand and board attention (§5, second paragraph), which treats $u$ as demand risk. That is a different construct from the model's, and the paper does not say which one its hypotheses concern. (iv) $m$, $\Phi$ and asset specificity all come from the same CIO survey. Hospital fixed effects do not remove time-varying reporting error or changes in the respondent's view. (v) A CIO may not know whether an interface is certified; the measure needs validation.
- *Repair.* Validate $m$ against an external source (certification records, vendor contracts, or a second respondent per hospital) and report reliability. Align the definitions: either measure modularity across all IT functions, or restate the model for clinical systems. State how $m=1$ is handled. Either use an uncertainty measure that matches the model (for example vendor-market turnover or technological change), or rename $u$ as demand volatility and reframe H2.

**M8. Sample integrity and inference.** Severity: `damaging` until resolved.

- *Commitment.* "a balanced panel of 412 U.S. general acute-care hospitals observed annually from 2009 to 2019", "yielding 4,712 hospital-year observations" (§3.1). T2 reports 4,712 observations and 412 hospitals for every model.
- *Gap.* A balanced panel of 412 hospitals over the eleven years 2009–2019 has $412\times11=4{,}532$ observations. The reported 4,712 is not a multiple of 412 ($4712/412\approx11.4$). If 2009 is dropped because $\text{Peer}_{t-1}$ is undefined, the maximum is $412\times10=4{,}120$. The panel is therefore unbalanced, covers different years, or the count is wrong. This is arithmetic, asserted until the data are checked, and it must be reconciled before anything else. Separately, the peer variable varies at the state-year level (§3.2), but standard errors are clustered by hospital (§3.3). Hospitals in the same state share state-year shocks, so hospital clustering understates the standard error of the peer coefficient. This affects the Model 1 peer effect, marked "***", and so the H3 comparison, in either direction. The number of states is not reported.
- *Repair.* Provide a sample flow (eligibility, missing years, lag loss, final N per model). Confirm that all four models use the same sample. Cluster by state, or use a wild-cluster bootstrap if the number of states is small, and report both. Report the number of states, the number of hospitals with any change in $m$, and the within-hospital SD of $m$ and $\Phi$, because the fixed-effects estimates use only that variation.

**M9. The moderation result is misreported, its interpretation is post hoc, and its implication is not stated.** Severity: `damaging`.

- *Commitment.* "contrary to H2. We return to this result in the discussion" (§4). "This interpretation is consistent with our theory" (§5, second paragraph). "strongly predicts" (Abstract).
- *Gap.* (i) T2, Model 3, reports the uncertainty main effect as "0.084*** (0.051)". The t-statistic is about 1.65 (p ≈ 0.10), so the three-star flag is wrong. (ii) In Model 3 the modularity effect at uncertainty $u$ is $0.118-0.061u$. It is zero at $u\approx1.9$ SD (Model 4: $0.097-0.058u$, zero at $u\approx1.7$). For hospitals with high uncertainty the association vanishes and, by extrapolation, reverses. The paper says nothing about this, and it bears directly on the claim that modularity is most valuable under uncertainty and on the advice in §5.1. The standard error of the marginal effect at high $u$ needs the covariance and should be reported. (iii) The legitimacy account in §5 is an institutional mechanism. It is offered to explain the paper's own main moderation result, while the Abstract and §6 state that institutional explanations are ruled out. It is not in the model and it is not tested. (iv) The negative sign is consistent with the threshold result under A3 (`cmt:M1`, `cmt:M2`), so it needs no legitimacy story. If the authors reject A3, the Discussion should say so and then test the story it relies on.
- *Repair.* Correct the stars. Report the marginal effect of modularity across the observed range of $u$, with intervals, and the share of hospital-years where it is indistinguishable from zero. Either test the signalling mechanism (for example, heterogeneity by public ownership, by public visibility, or by board structure), or drop it and state the result as the model's prediction under A3. Reconcile the conclusion with the mixed evidence.

## 3. Minor concerns

**m1. Claims and terms.** "Strongly predicts" overstates an association that adds about 0.05 to within-R² (Model 1: 0.08; Model 2: 0.13) and equals about one-sixth of an SD of outsourcing (§4, H1). The Abstract refers to "prior adoption by peer hospitals"; the variable is an outsourcing share, not adoption. "The first study to adjudicate directly" is not supportable without engaging the literature (`cmt:m2`). Repair: align the terms with the variables and remove the priority claim unless it can be substantiated.

**m2. Literature.** The paper does not engage prior work on diffusion and influence in IT outsourcing (for example, Loh and Venkatraman, 1992 (to verify)), or on transaction-cost explanations of IT outsourcing (for example, Ang and Straub, 1998 (to verify)). It also does not engage the modularity and boundary literature beyond Langlois (2002) and Sanchez and Mahoney (1996) (for example, Langlois and Robertson, 1992, and Jacobides and Winter, 2005 (both to verify)). Repair: engage these works and position the contribution against them.

**m3. Model completeness.** Assumptions A1 and A2 are not stated, although the one stated is numbered A3. The distribution of $k$, the spend weights and the treatment of $m=1$ should be given. Repair: list all primitives and assumptions.

**m4. Proposition 1 is built into the functional form.** $m$ enters only through $(1-m)^{-1}$ (Appendix, Proof of Proposition 1), so the sign follows once $s+G>0$ and $H>0$. This is a comparative static, not an insight beyond the form. Repair: say so, or derive the result in a setting where modularity affects more than the hold-up term.

**m5. Static model, diffusion in the title.** The model is a one-period comparative static with no dynamics, no network and no learning. It cannot speak to the time path or spread of outsourcing. Repair: qualify the title and claims, or add dynamics if diffusion is the claim.

**m6. Unreported robustness.** "Results are similar when the outsourcing share is computed over all IT spending including licences, and when the sample is restricted to non-system hospitals" (§4, Robustness). These results need a table or appendix with coefficients.

**m7. Effect sizes and standard deviations.** The "one-sixth of a standard deviation" calculation (§4, H1) uses an SD that is not identified. State whether 0.24 is the total or the within-hospital SD, since the fixed-effects estimate uses within variation. Report asset specificity effects per SD as well as per full range.

**m8. Selection from the balanced design.** Requiring survey response in all eleven years selects on survey engagement and excludes hospitals that merged, closed or changed systems, which are plausibly the hospitals that re-sourced IT. §5.2 notes over-representation of large hospitals but not this selection. Repair: report attrition, compare responders and non-responders on observables, and rerun the main model on an unbalanced panel.

**m9. Functional form.** The outsourcing share is bounded in $[0,1]$, and a linear fixed-effects model can produce out-of-range predictions. Report the range of fitted values and a fractional-response robustness check (for example, Papke and Wooldridge, 1996 (to verify)).

**m10. Notation and wording.** (a) In §4, H1 is "strongly and positively associated ... in every specification", but Model 1 omits modularity. (b) The index $s$ in $\text{Peer}_{s,t-1}$ (§3.3) is not defined there. (c) The appendix sentence "the cross-partial is determined by how the numerator responds to uncertainty" is incorrect (`cmt:M1`). (d) §3.3 describes "culture and governance" as stable, although system membership, which the paper controls for, changes over time.

## 4. Recommendation

**Reject in the current form, with encouragement to resubmit a substantially revised manuscript.**

The central theoretical proposition is wrong as proved. Under the paper's own Assumption A3 its sign is the reverse of the one announced in the Abstract, the Introduction and H2 (`cmt:M1`). The paper's empirical moderation result has the sign that the corrected threshold model implies, yet the paper treats it as contrary evidence and explains it with a mechanism that is outside the model (`cmt:M9`). The headline claim that mimicry is ruled out rests on a non-significant coefficient whose interval does not exclude a large effect, and on conditioning on a variable that may be the very channel of the effect under test (`cmt:M4`). The causal language about modularity has no first stage and an incompletely reported event study (`cmt:M5`). The sample count does not match the stated design (`cmt:M8`). None of these is cosmetic. Repairing them changes the conclusions, not only the wording.

The question is worth answering, and the H1 association may survive. A resubmission could be a real contribution if it (i) corrects the theory and reconciles it with the data, (ii) tests the mediation channel and peer × uncertainty, (iii) establishes the causal claim or restricts it, and (iv) decomposes the 2009–2019 rise. I would not recommend major revision as the next step, because the required changes amount to new analysis and a new argument rather than a revision of this one. If the handling editor prefers to keep the manuscript in play, the minimum conditions for a further round are `cmt:M1`, `cmt:M4`, `cmt:M8` and `cmt:M9`.
