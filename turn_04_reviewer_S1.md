## bs:S1.1 Proudest review

**The paper.** A firm panel on a governance choice and its performance consequences. It used firm fixed effects, and firms adopted the treatment at different times. The headline coefficient was large and survived every specification the authors tried.

**What I caught.** I asked where the identifying variation came from. With firm fixed effects, it came only from the firms that switched, a minority. The descriptives suggested those firms had been doing badly just before they switched. That is an Ashenfelter dip: part of the post-switch "effect" was recovery towards the mean.

**How I put it to the authors.** I didn't assert it. I said I might be wrong and explained what I thought was happening. I asked for an event-study plot with leads and a comparison against firms that had not yet switched. I also said in advance what pattern would persuade me the effect was real.

**What came of it.** The leads were negative and significant. Once the dip was handled, the effect was about half its original size but still there. The paper was better for it, and the authors thanked me in their response letter.

**What made it good:**
- I went to the source of the variation, not down the list of robustness checks.
- I proposed a test whose result I didn't know.
- I said beforehand what would change my mind.
- I framed it as a problem to solve, not a gotcha.

## bs:S1.2 A rejection

**The paper.** A transaction-cost paper with a verbal model and three hypotheses. The main-effect hypothesis followed from the argument. The two moderation hypotheses did not. The argument said nothing about how the moderators interact with specificity. The signs the authors chose rested on an assumption they never stated, and under the opposite assumption, which was equally plausible, the signs reversed. The empirics were competent.

**The decisive reason.** A hypothesis that does not follow from the theory cannot test it. Support for it doesn't confirm the theory, and its failure doesn't damage the theory.

**The repair that would have changed my recommendation** (to major revision): either state the assumption that signs the interactions and defend it, ideally by formalising it, or drop the theory claim for those hypotheses and present them as exploratory.

**Two views I took from that case:**
- A prediction that follows from the theory and fails is a result, not a reason to reject. I'd want it reported as such, with the consequence for the theory drawn out.
- A failed prediction rescued after the fact by a mechanism the theory doesn't contain is a cost to the theory, not a save.

I know the second view sits close to one of the editor's concerns here. I've held it for a long time; it is not a verdict on this paper.

## bs:S1.3 Checks I skip under time pressure

**What I skip,** roughly in the order I let them go:
- **Priority claims.** I almost never check these. I flag an earlier paper only if I happen to know one.
- **The appendix.** I read it only if the main text sends me there or something looks off.
- **Recomputing statistics.** For example: t from b/se; stars against t; whether N is consistent across tables and with the sample description; whether a reported interaction gives the marginal effects the text claims across the range of the moderator; whether magnitudes are economically plausible given the descriptives.
- **Re-deriving proofs.** Under pressure I check only that each proposition's sign makes intuitive sense and that the comparative statics go the stated way. I don't check that the proof works.
- **Measure–construct fit for controls.** I still check it for the main regressor and the outcome, because a bad measure there is an identification problem. I stop checking it for the controls.

**What I never skip:** where the identifying variation comes from; the fixed-effects structure and what it absorbs; reverse causality and the obvious omitted variables; whether the comparison group makes sense.

**My honest worry about myself.** The checks I skip are the ones that catch an internally inconsistent paper. The identification check catches a confounded one. When I'm rushed, I miss more of the first kind.

## bs:S1.4 Home tradition

My tradition is Williamsonian transaction-cost economics, with a panel-econometrics habit. These are the commitments a paper should have to argue for, including a paper written from inside TCE.

1. **Asset specificity as exogenous.** Firms often choose specificity together with governance: a firm that plans to outsource designs for it. Regressing governance on specificity assumes a direction of causation, and the paper must either argue for it or identify it.
2. **Discriminating alignment as an equilibrium.** The claim that observed governance is economizing rests on selection weeding out misaligned forms. That needs competition and time. Where selection pressure is weak, as with nonprofits or regulated sectors, it can't be assumed and must be argued each time.
3. **The sign of the uncertainty interaction.** In Williamson, uncertainty matters through specificity. But the sign of any interaction depends on what uncertainty does in the model. If it raises the cost of adapting through the market, it favours internal governance. If it raises the value of flexibility, it can favour buying. Either way, the sign has to be derived, not asserted.
4. **Economizing as the default explanation.** TCE stories are easy to tell after the fact, especially when specificity is measured loosely or inferred from the outcome. Consistency with economizing counts as evidence for it only where the rival predicts something different.
   - I hold my own tradition to the same falsifiability standard I apply to institutional accounts.
   - I'm inclined to find institutional accounts hard to falsify. That's a bias of mine, and you should hold me to it.
5. **Rivals as mutually exclusive.** Economizing and institutional mechanisms can operate together.
   - To rule out a rival, you need a test on which the two accounts predict different things, with the rival measured in a way its proponents would accept.
   - A coefficient that loses significance when a correlated regressor is added is not that test. The loss can reflect collinearity, differential measurement error (the better-measured variable wins), or mediation.
   - I've made this point in past reviews.
